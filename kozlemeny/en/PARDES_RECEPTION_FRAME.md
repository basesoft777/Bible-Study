## What the level means, and what it does not

On the pages, a PaRDeS level may be attached to every motif, every occurrence and every relation between passages: **Peshat** (the plain, literal reading), **Remez** (allusion: recognition of the connection, without inference), **Derash** (inferential, applied reading) or **Sod** (spiritual reading, only as derivable from the text, without numerology or allegorization). One passage may carry several levels; where a passage is not classified, the field is empty. The level of a motif states the motif's centre of gravity.

(The Hungarian-language pages and data fields use the spellings *Pshat*, *Remez*, *Drash*, *Sod*; this English text follows the common scholarly transliteration *Peshat*, *Derash*.)

The level marks a **mode of reading**, not the meaning of the text. It says in what kind of reading a passage connects to the motif: literally, as allusion, as inference or as spiritual reading. It **does not claim** that the biblical author intended this meaning, and it is not a level of the text's original, historical meaning.

The categories come from the fourfold scheme of reading in Jewish exegesis, so the level classifies in the language of the tradition's modes of reading: *this is how it can be read, and how the tradition read it*, not *this is what the text means*. The classification itself, however, is made by this work, according to its own rules. How a named interpreter actually read a given passage is documented not by the level but by the page's section on named-teacher matches (Tanítói egyezés), with a source, where that step was run.

## Why this framing matters

The fourfold scheme is medieval: the acronym appears in medieval Jewish exegesis, and it is akin to the fourfold sense of Scripture in the Christian Middle Ages. Reading the level as a claim about the original meaning of the biblical text would be anachronistic, and critical biblical scholarship would rightly object. As a reception label, by contrast, it classifies *how* a passage connects to the motif, and the classification is retrievable. The evidence that a passage is an occurrence of a motif is never the level but the Hebrew or Greek text (see below).

## The evidence is the Hebrew and Greek text, not the translation

Whether a passage belongs to the motif is decided by the Hebrew and Greek text: the motif's shared words by Strong's number, the word pairs, the verb forms and the Septuagint correspondence. The Károli word (from the classic Hungarian Protestant Bible) appears at every occurrence, but it is **display data**: the window through which the Hungarian reader sees the passage. For every Károli word the page states how the alignment with the Hebrew or Greek word was made and how confident it is.

The Károli layer therefore counts in two ways. For the Hungarian reader and for the history of Hungarian translation it is data in its own right: it shows how the Hungarian Protestant Bible rendered the motif's words. In international exegetical argument, however, it is not evidence; there the credibility of a motif rests only on the text in the original languages.
